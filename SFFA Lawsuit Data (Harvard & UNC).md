# SFFA v. Harvard & SFFA v. UNC — Disclosed Admissions Data

> **What this is:** Factual data points that became public through *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* and *Students for Fair Admissions v. University of North Carolina* — court exhibits, expert reports, and trial testimony, plus the district court and Supreme Court rulings. Both cases were decided together by the Supreme Court on June 29, 2023 (6-3), ending race-conscious admissions at both schools. This is a one-time historical disclosure — nothing comparable exists for any other school in this archive, since no other university's internal admissions weighting has been forced into public record.
>
> **How to read this file — confidence labels matter more here than anywhere else in the project:**
> - **[Established]** — Harvard's or UNC's own produced data, or a court's factual finding, reported consistently across primary documents and multiple first-tier outlets.
> - **[Modeled / contested]** — output of one side's statistical/expert model (usually SFFA's expert, economist Peter Arcidiacono), which the opposing side's expert and, in most instances, the trial judge did not fully credit. These numbers are real and specific, but they are *a model's output*, not an adjudicated fact — the case record contains dueling models, not a single agreed number.
> - Conflating "raw admit rate" (undisputed, from the school's own produced dataset) with "regression-adjusted counterfactual" (a contested model's projection of what *would* happen under a different policy) is the single most common error in secondary reporting on this case. This file keeps them in separate sections deliberately.
>
> No political framing or advocacy is intended or included below — only the disclosed numbers, their sources, and which side produced them.

## 1. Harvard — raw admit rates by race (Classes of 2000–2017, i.e. 1995–2013 admissions cycles)

**[Established]** — from a Harvard-produced dataset covering 18 admissions cycles, released and reported during the October 2018 trial.

| Group | Raw admit rate |
|:--|--:|
| Asian American | 8.1% |
| White | 11.1% |
| Hispanic | 10.6% |
| African American | 13.2% |

These are unconditional admit rates, not adjusted for academic strength. Asian American applicants had the lowest raw admit rate of the four groups despite the highest average SAT scores of any group over the same period — this gap was the starting point of SFFA's case.

*Source: The Harvard Crimson, ["Asian-American Harvard Applicants Saw Lowest Admit Rate of Any Racial Group From 1995 to 2013"](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2018/10/19/acceptance-rates-by-race/) (Oct. 19, 2018), reporting directly off Harvard's court-produced dataset; companion piece on test scores, ["Asian-American Harvard Admits Earned Highest Average SAT Score of Any Racial Group From 1995 to 2013"](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2018/10/22/asian-american-admit-sat-scores/).*

## 2. Harvard — ALDC preferences (Athletes, Legacies, Dean's/Director's-interest list, Children of faculty/staff)

**[Established]** — the most robustly multi-sourced figures in the entire case, drawn from economists Arcidiacono & Kinsler's analysis of Harvard's own Classes-of-2014–2019 data, published as [NBER Working Paper w26316](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26316/w26316.pdf) and in the *Journal of Labor Economics* 40(1).

| Applicant category | Admit rate |
|:--|--:|
| Recruited athlete | ~86% |
| Dean's/Director's interest list (major-donor-connected) | ~42% |
| Child of faculty/staff | ~46–47% |
| Legacy | ~33–34% |
| Non-ALDC baseline | under 5.5–6% |

Harvard's overall admit rate across this period ran roughly 4.6%–6.3% depending on the exact class year (falling from ~5.9% for the Class of 2018 to ~4.6% by the Class of 2023, per Harvard's own OIR admissions statistics).

**Who benefited:** **43% of admitted white students (Classes of 2014–2019) were ALDC**, versus **under 16%** of admitted African American, Hispanic, and Asian American students combined. Roughly three-quarters of white ALDC admits were estimated to be applicants who would likely have been rejected without that status. Over the longer span from the Class of 2000 to the Class of 2017, the ALDC admit-rate advantage grew from roughly 4x to roughly 9x the non-ALDC rate, even as ALDC applicants shrank from ~7.5% to ~4% of the applicant pool — while still making up roughly 24% of every admitted class throughout.

*Source: Harvard Crimson, ["Legacy Admit Rate Five Times That of Non-Legacies, Court Docs Show"](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2018/6/20/admissions-docs-legacy/) (June 20, 2018) and ["Legacy, Athlete, and Donor Preferences Disproportionately Benefit White Applicants, per Analysis"](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2019/10/23/nber-admissions-data/) (Oct. 23, 2019); underlying paper hosted by SFFA at [studentsforfairadmissions.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/legacyathlete.pdf](https://studentsforfairadmissions.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/legacyathlete.pdf).*

## 3. Harvard — personal rating disparities

**[Established for the raw percentages; interpretation of *why* is disputed]**

Harvard's internal file-review process scored every applicant on a 1–6 scale across four dimensions: Academic, Extracurricular, Athletic, and **Personal** (character/likability, assessed by admissions readers, not by the applicant's own materials).

- **21.3% of white applicants** received a top personal-rating score (1 or 2), versus **17.6% of Asian American applicants**, over the six studied admissions cycles.
- Across the same cycles, Asian American applicants had the lowest average personal rating of the four groups (Black highest, then Hispanic, then white, then Asian American).
- Harvard's own dean of admissions, William Fitzsimmons, testified that part of the gap might reflect weaker guidance-counselor and teacher recommendation letters for Asian American applicants relative to other groups — his explanation, not an independently verified cause.
- **[Modeled/contested]** For applicants in the top academic decile specifically, one SFFA-side analysis found white applicants roughly 40% more likely than Asian American applicants in the same decile to receive a high personal rating — this is Arcidiacono's figure, not a court-adopted one.

**What the court concluded:** Judge Burroughs found the personal-rating gap real in the data, but ruled it did not reflect intentional racial discrimination or "racial animus," while not fully endorsing Harvard's process as flawless either. The Supreme Court's 2023 majority opinion did not resolve the personal-rating question directly — it decided the case on broader equal-protection grounds and left that specific factual dispute essentially undecided.

*Sources: NBC News, ["Lawsuit accuses Harvard of discriminating against Asian American applicants in personal ratings"](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/asian-america/lawsuit-accuses-harvard-discriminating-against-asian-american-applicants-personal-ratings-n884476); CNN, ["Harvard ranks Asian-Americans lower on personality traits, lawsuit alleges"](https://www.cnn.com/2018/06/15/politics/harvard-admissions-asian-american/index.html) (June 15, 2018); Harvard Crimson retrospective, ["Did Harvard Intentionally Discriminate? In Admissions Discrimination Suit, the Supreme Court Doesn't Say"](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/2023/7/20/sffa-decision-asian-american-discrimination/) (July 20, 2023).*

## 4. Harvard — SFFA's regression-adjusted "counterfactual" model (contested)

**[Modeled/contested — SFFA's expert output, not a court-adopted finding]**

Peter Arcidiacono's report for SFFA, based on ~160,000+ applicant records across roughly six admissions cycles (Classes of 2014–2019), modeled what admit rates *would* look like holding academic/extracurricular/athletic profile constant and varying only the applicant's race/sex:

- A modeled "average" Asian American applicant had roughly a **25%** predicted admission chance — versus **~30%** if the same profile were modeled as a white female applicant, and **~36%** as a white male applicant.
- Modeled admit-rate multiplier relative to being treated as white: African American applicants' predicted rate was **more than 4x** higher; Hispanic applicants' **~2.4x** higher.
- Under a simulated "academics-only" admissions model, Asian Americans were projected to rise to **~43%** of the admitted class, versus roughly 24% actual share at the time.
- Removing the modeled "personal rating penalty" was estimated to raise the Asian American admit rate by roughly **19%**.

**This model was not adopted by the trial court.** Judge Burroughs explicitly found that SFFA's statistical model "overstates the direct effect of Asian American identity on the personal rating" and that there was "no credible evidence that corroborates the improper discrimination suggested by [SFFA's] statistical model," crediting Harvard's expert David Card's competing analysis instead. Treat every number in this section as one economist's contested model output, presented at trial and then rejected by the fact-finder.

*Source: Arcidiacono, Kinsler & Ransom, ["Asian American Discrimination in Harvard Admissions,"](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27068/w27068.pdf) NBER Working Paper w27068; SFFA-hosted copy at [studentsforfairadmissions.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/realpenalty.pdf](https://studentsforfairadmissions.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/realpenalty.pdf); Harvard's competing expert report (David Card), [projects.iq.harvard.edu — expert_report_-_2017-12-15...pdf](https://projects.iq.harvard.edu/files/diverse-education/files/expert_report_-_2017-12-15_dr._david_card_expert_report_updated_confid_desigs_redacted.pdf).*

## 5. Case outcomes

**District court — Judge Allison Burroughs, Sept. 30, 2019:** After a 15-day bench trial (Oct. 15 – Nov. 2, 2018), ruled for Harvard on all four counts (no intentional discrimination; no disparate impact via the personal rating; race used lawfully as a "plus" factor; no racial balancing). Affirmed by the 1st Circuit in Nov. 2020.

*Sources: [Official ruling text (Harvard-hosted)](https://www.harvard.edu/admissionscase/ruling); Harvard Gazette, ["Judge upholds Harvard's admissions policy"](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2019/10/judge-upholds-harvards-admissions-policy/) (Oct. 1, 2019).*

**Supreme Court, June 29, 2023 (6-3; Justice Jackson recused from the Harvard case, dissented in the UNC case):** Held that both Harvard's and UNC's race-conscious admissions programs violate equal-protection principles (the 14th Amendment for UNC as a public university; Title VI for Harvard as a federal-funding recipient), because the programs lacked sufficiently focused and measurable objectives, used race as a negative factor for some applicants, relied on racial stereotyping, and lacked a logical endpoint.

Specific figures the majority opinion drew from the trial record's race-neutral simulation models (Class of 2019, depending on which simulation):
- Under race-neutral simulations, African American admits would fall by **at least 7 percentage points**, Hispanic admits by **at least 4 points**, while Asian American admits would rise by **at least 3 points** and white admits by **6–8 points**.
- At least **10% of Harvard's admitted class** — including more than **one-third of admitted Hispanic students** and more than **half of admitted African American students** — likely would not have been admitted under a race-neutral process, per the trial record.
- Considering race was associated with roughly an **11% decrease** in the number of Asian American admits relative to a race-blind counterfactual.

*Source: [Full opinion, SFFA v. Harvard / SFFA v. UNC, No. 20-1199 (supremecourt.gov PDF)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/20-1199_hgdj.pdf).*

## 6. UNC-Chapel Hill

**District court — Judge Loretta Biggs, Oct. 18, 2021 (later reversed on appeal to the Supreme Court, consolidated with the Harvard case):** After an 8-day bench trial (Nov. 2020), ruled UNC's process survived strict scrutiny, while the opinion itself documented real disparities:

**[Established, from Judge Biggs's own opinion]** Among in-state, top-academic-decile applicants specifically: **3% of Black candidates were denied**, versus **1.2% of white** and **1.8% of Asian American** candidates in the same decile — notably, in this narrow top-academic band, Black applicants had a *slightly lower* admit rate than white or Asian American applicants, an unusual and frequently cited counter-data-point to the broader pattern. Biggs explicitly declined to rely purely on statistical modeling, writing that such analysis "misses important context to include obscuring racial barriers and obstacles that have been faced, overcome and are yet to be overcome."

**[Modeled/contested — SFFA's expert output, explicitly found "far less credible" by Judge Biggs]** Arcidiacono, Kinsler & Ransom's UNC-specific model ([NBER Working Paper w29964](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29964/w29964.pdf)), comparing applicants at the fifth decile of UNC's academic index:
- In-state: modeled African American admit-rate advantage ~2.55x over Asian American, ~2.45x over white, ~35% higher than Hispanic; overall in-state African American admit-rate increase attributable to racial preference estimated at over 70% relative to a race-neutral counterfactual.
- Out-of-state: far larger modeled gaps — African American admit rate roughly **33x** higher than Asian American, **>14x** higher than white, **~2.5x** higher than Hispanic in the same decile.
- Out-of-state Hispanic applicants had the single highest raw admit rate in this analysis, ~20.18%, about 85% higher than white out-of-state applicants in the same comparison.
- UNC's own experts, per case-summary sources, contended race explained only 1–6% of admissions decisions depending on model specification.

**No UNC-specific ALDC-style breakdown found.** Unlike Harvard, UNC's litigation record did not surface a discrete, well-sourced "legacy admit rate" or "athlete admit rate" analogous to Harvard's 33%/86% figures. If that's needed later, the UNC trial exhibits (hosted at [admissionslawsuit.unc.edu](https://admissionslawsuit.unc.edu/)) or the SCOTUSblog case file would be the place to look — flagged here as an open gap rather than presenting an unverified number.

*Sources: Washington Post, ["University of North Carolina can use affirmative action, Judge Loretta Biggs rules"](https://www.washingtonpost.com/education/2021/10/18/affirmative-action-university-of-north-carolina/) (Oct. 18, 2021); CNN coverage, [cnn.com/2021/10/19/us/unc-race-in-admissions-lawsuit](https://www.cnn.com/2021/10/19/us/unc-race-in-admissions-lawsuit/index.html); College Board ADC case summary noting the "far less credible" finding, [highered.collegeboard.org — adc-analysis-sffa-vs-unc-case-summary.pdf](https://highered.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/adc-analysis-sffa-vs-unc-case-summary.pdf).*

## 8. How this connects to the rest of the archive

Nothing in `combined_profiles.json` or `school_facts.json` can be cross-checked against this file directly — the archive has no per-applicant race/legacy/athlete labels, and neither Harvard nor UNC disclosed data recent enough (post-2023 ruling) to reflect the current race-blind admissions regime both schools now operate under. This file is best used as **structural context** for reading any Harvard or UNC profile in the archive: a stated "legacy" or "recruited athlete" status in a profile's narrative should be read against the base rates above (Harvard: ~33% legacy, ~86% athlete, vs. a low-single-digit rate for otherwise-unhooked applicants in the same era), while keeping in mind that both schools' processes changed after June 2023 and these specific numbers predate that change.
